HOW THIS EXAMPLE WAS DECIDED

Read it against the input beside it. Its subject is a working harbour ferry.
Yours is not. Copy the reasoning, never the vessel, the crews or the
sentences.

WHAT THE FOUNDATION ALREADY DECIDED

The foundation fixed the ferry: two identical working ends, gates along
both sides, benches across the width, a helm cabin at mid-length above a
crew stair. None of that is chosen again here. Every scene shows part of
it, and no scene adds a part of the ferry the foundation does not name.

The characters and the locations were declared before this step and are
used here by their ids. No scene adds, renames or redescribes one. The
crew stair is not a location: the foundation placed it, and the scenes keep
it where it was.

PEOPLE WITHOUT IDS

The profile's cast is protagonist_only, so the ferry is the only
character. The design team, the yard crews, the fitting-out crews, the
operator representative, the master, the deck hands and the passengers are
written into the action by role, each with the dress or the things they
carry that make them recognizable, and described the same way whenever
they return. They never appear in character_ids and nobody speaks.
character_ids holds the ferry wherever it is present and is empty in the
design scene, where only a paper strip stands for it.

THE THIRTY-SECOND FILM

The film is three segments of 10000 ms. The scenes fill them in order:
sc_01, sc_02 and sc_03 are segment 1, from the paper strip to the finished
hull on its blocks; sc_04 and sc_05 are segment 2, the launch and the first
boarding; sc_06 and sc_07 are segment 3, the crossing and the ending. Each
segment's durations add up to exactly 10000: 3000, 4000 and 3000, then
5000 and 5000, then 5000 and 5000. The durations are not equal by rule:
the framing of the whole hull gets more time than the paper strip, and the
launch, which the viewer has to watch happen, gets five seconds. Each
segment ends where the story turns: the finished hull, then the ferry in
service.

Each scene shows one moment the viewer can read in its time. sc_02 keeps
two moments of weeks of framing, the matching end frames and the roof over
open bays, never every operation. Every scene has one camera sentence: where
the camera stands and how it moves, something a crew could do there, such
as the top of a staging tower or a bench under way. Camera words appear in
no other field.

FINISHED BEFORE THE LAUNCH

The ferry is coated and finished on its blocks in the dry dock. sc_03 hangs
the gates and coats the hull while it stands there. The benches and rails
are fitted inside the hall off screen, between sc_03 and sc_04, because the
brief leaves work inside the hall off screen; coverage names both scenes,
and sc_04 starts with them in place. sc_04 is the launch, its own scene:
the dock is flooded by the culverts the dock declares, the ferry floats off
its blocks and is warped out through the dock gate. No scene before it has
the ferry afloat, and none after it adds a finish. The handover to the
operator is also off screen, between sc_04 and sc_05.

THE DESIGN ON SCREEN

The ferry carries its profile, and the scenes keep its positions: the
gates are on the port and starboard sides, the helm cabin on the roof at
mid-length. Each signature feature is shown doing something rather than
named: the gates are hung and slid shut in the dock, kept closed through
the launch, opened by the master at the pier and kept closed against the
spray.

The side gates are the feature the foundation makes central, so their
whole course stays in the film: the team marks them along the paper strip
in sc_01, the sides rise as openings in sc_02, the gates are hung and slid
shut in sc_03, they stay closed as the ferry floats out in sc_04, a crowd
passes through them in sc_05, they keep the spray out in sc_06, and in
sc_07 the passengers wait at them for the next pier.

Whenever people use the gates the order is the same: they open, people pass
through, everyone is clear of the gate line, and only then do they close.
sc_05 writes the whole order and says who works the gates: the master from
the helm cabin.

WHAT EARNS A SCENE

Each scene gives the viewer something the scenes before it did not: the
rule of the width in sc_01, the matching ends and open sides in sc_02, the
sides closing into one surface in sc_03, the finished ferry afloat and
leaving the yard in sc_04, a whole crowd boarding at once in sc_05, shelter
under way in sc_06, and the passengers already standing where the side
will open in sc_07.

sc_06 changes nothing physical. It stays because the ending needs a pause
in which the closed side is simply lived beside; a pause that lets a
feeling land earns its place as much as a piece of work.

HOW STAGE TREATMENTS BECAME SCENES

The design treatment became one scene. The construction treatment became
one montage in the building dock. Finishing is one montage on the hull on
its blocks, in the same dock. Completion is the launch. Operation became
three scenes: the boarding at the home pier, then the crossing and the
arrival at the far pier that carry the foundation's ending. The count
follows what each treatment needs and what thirty seconds can hold, not a
quota per stage and not the coverage list.

Each handover_to_next reappears as the leads_to of the last scene of its
stage. The last scene realizes the foundation's ending and leads nowhere.

CONTINUOUS SCENES AND MONTAGES

A continuous scene holds only what can happen while the viewer watches: a
team round a table, a crossing, an arrival. Framing a hull takes weeks, so
sc_02 is a montage; so are sc_03, where the coating follows the gates over
days, and sc_04, where the dock takes hours to fill. sc_05 is a montage of
minutes: the side opening as the crowd starts across and the side closing
in front of a full hall.

The beat that follows a gap says in time_jump what was done in the gap,
and never the work that beat goes on to do. In sc_03, b2 begins "Days
later, with every gate hung, the hull and roof coated" and goes on to the
hand along the seams.

Each beat is a development of the story, not a shot, and no beat carries a
camera, a framing or a cut.

SUBJECT STATE

The design scene carries null: the ferry is not shown. Every later scene
records the ferry as it opens and as it closes, so continuity can be
checked scene by scene. Each start follows the end of the scene before it:
sc_03 starts exactly where sc_02 ended, and sc_04 starts with the benches
and rails that the transition between them accounts for.

Progress and configuration are kept apart. Progress says what is built and
where the ferry stands. Configuration says only which parts that open or
close a space matter and what state they are in: the gates are not listed
before sc_03 hangs them. The ferry's permanent design is in neither; it
comes from its profile.

ROOM STATE

The ferry hall is the one place aboard, and it is filmed only in use, so
sc_06 and sc_07 are fitted from start to end. No scene is set in the hall
while work goes on there. Every scene away from the ferry, in the studio,
the building dock and at the pier, carries null for both.

LIGHT, WEATHER AND PROPS

Each scene's light comes from the light sources and openings its location
declares: the studio's windows and lamps, the dock's roof lights and
floodlights, with daylight spreading in through the gate as it opens, and
the hall's glazed gates. Weather appears only where the place is open to
the sky or seen through an opening, and the spray of the crossing belongs
to sc_06.

Props are the loose things the action handles: the paper strip and card
benches in sc_01, the template in sc_02, the mooring lines in sc_04, the
passengers' bags and newspapers later. The gates and benches are never
props, because they are the ferry; the crane, the staging and the dock gate
are never props, because the location fixes them.

COVERAGE

Every item the profile declares is answered once. Required items point at
the scenes that show them: cov_comp_launch at sc_04. cov_fin_benches and
cov_comp_handover are off screen in the brief, so each is a transition
naming the scene before and the scene after, never shown.

The film_brief lists the passenger hall, so the profile adds
cov_space_passenger_hall. It is shown by sc_06 and sc_07, both set at
lo_ferry_hall, the location that carries brief_space "passenger_hall". A
pier scene outside the hall could not carry it.

THE LOCATIONS SERVE THE SCENES

The scenes were worked out from what happens, and each was then placed
where its action happens. The locations did not set the list.
lo_building_dock returns three times, each time for something new: the
framing, the finishing on the blocks and the launch. lo_harbour and
lo_far_pier have no scene: the ferry is seen leaving through the gate from
inside the dock, and the arrival at the far pier is seen from inside the
hall.

A MOMENT, NOT A ROUND TRIP

The rooms are shown through what happens in them, not through people
arriving and leaving. sc_06 is already out on open water with the
passengers seated; nobody walks in. sc_07 ends as the ferry comes
alongside, before anyone leaves.

SCALE IS NOT A TARGET

This ferry needs seven scenes in thirty seconds. Another subject may need
more or fewer, never more than the profile's max_scenes. Your count comes
from your foundation and from what can be seen in thirty seconds.

WHAT THIS STEP DOES NOT DO

There is no logline, design thesis, premise, synopsis, stage treatment or
ending in the answer: the application keeps the foundation's versions.
There are no characters or locations: the application keeps the declared
ones. There is no dialogue, because nobody is declared who could speak.
There are no shot lists and no measurements, and camera words appear only
in each scene's camera field.
