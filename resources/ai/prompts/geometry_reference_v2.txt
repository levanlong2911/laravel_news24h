PRIORITY SYSTEM

P0 - IDENTITY: the smallest set of features whose loss would cause the object to become a different design.
P1 - COUNT / TOPOLOGY: exact counts and structural relationships - number of primary volumes; major tiers; wings; principal openings; supports; terraces; relationships between components; containment; connectivity; symmetry.
P2 - PRIMARY GEOMETRY: shape of major components and how they transition.
P3 - PROPORTION: length, width, height, beam, wheelbase, span, deck height, mass ratios.
P4 - SECONDARY GEOMETRY: openings, apertures, recesses, seams, structural edges, and glazing or rails only where the chosen subject state includes them, plus subordinate permanent features.

Exact counts come only from the source. Once a source count is used, preserve it consistently throughout the prompt.

TOPOLOGY BEFORE DECORATION

Describe the whole subject's exterior form first: for a vessel, the hull in profile and in plan, the bow, the sides, the stern, and how the upper volumes stand on the hull; for another object, its overall body and how its main parts attach to it. Only then describe courts, decks, openings and moving parts. Use what the source states about that form; where it says little, say little rather than invent.

Always establish what components exist; how many exist; where they are; what connects to what; what contains what; what overlaps what; what is recessed; what projects; what is continuous; what terminates; what is symmetrical - before describing finish or aesthetic appearance.

Describe each connecting element by its endpoints. Any axis term must use the object's coordinate system and agree with those endpoints.

A beautiful image with incorrect topology is a failed geometry reference.

REFERENCE STATE

Choose the visual state that exposes permanent geometry most clearly.

Plating is bare unpainted metal (the source's material when it names one), matte and light-absorbing, with restrained surface marking, broad weak highlights and visible weld seams and frame lines. CAMERA STRATEGY

The master view is always ONE oblique view: a three-quarter view or a side profile. Never use a plan view from above or a head-on view of either end as the master view, even when the source's proof views list them; those belong to later reference views.

Superyacht: the master view is an elevated three-quarter view from the bow, high enough to read the deck levels, the fine entry and how the superstructure stands on the hull, with moderate telephoto perspective. When the design context gives reference_proof_requirements, use them only to choose the side: take the port or starboard bow three-quarter view that proves more P0 targets, and port when they are equal. Name in CAMERA only the P0 targets that view proves; the rest are left to later reference views and are never forced into this one.

Other subjects: choose ONE oblique master view from the subject's silhouette and identity-critical geometry; do not default to either end of the subject. Use a side/profile view when longitudinal proportion is substantially more important than depth information. Use an elevated three-quarter view only when upper-surface topology is identity-critical.

Every constrained feature stays described in the geometry sections. CAMERA, FRAMING and LIGHTING must not require a feature to be visible when the chosen view would naturally occlude it. In CAMERA, name only the faces and features the chosen view shows, each identified by its side (port or starboard, forward or aft); natural occlusion of the rest is accepted and needs no compensation. CAMERA, FRAMING and LIGHTING refer only to shapes the geometry sections already state: they never introduce a new shape, such as a hull curvature the source does not describe, and never ask to clarify a feature the chosen view cannot see. Prioritize silhouette and identity-critical relationships; do not distort or relocate geometry to expose hidden features. Integrated structure is described as integrated, not as a separate visible element.

Avoid extreme perspective. Prefer moderate telephoto or normal perspective; low optical distortion; sufficient camera distance; full-object visibility; comparable scale between near and far ends.

BACKGROUND

On bare or unfinished metal, keep the specular response low and diffuse so the surface reads as matte material rather than polished sheet.
