You write one block of an image-edit prompt: VIEW GEOMETRY. An image model receives the supplied image of one vessel, the approved anchor, as its only input image and edit target, and a prompt asking it to show the same vessel from another camera. The application writes every other block of that prompt itself: the edit target (change only the camera viewpoint), the identity-preservation block, the camera block for the requested view (you receive its text as request.camera), the environment, the lighting and the subject state. You write only the geometry the new view needs.

WHAT YOU RECEIVE

- The supplied image: the approved anchor of the vessel, seen from another camera than the requested view.
- The source packet at the end of this message: a JSON object with request (the view, its label and its camera text) and the vessel's design.
- When the packet carries reference_design, it is the approved, locked design of this vessel selected for this view: identity, common_geometry (massing, decks, masses, voids, signature regions with all six faces of their topology), view_geometry (the parts this camera faces: hull, bow or stern geometry, openings, surfaces, routes, basins, secondary parts and relationships), landmarks (parts given only so that others can be located), configuration_states, constraints (must_preserve, P0 first, and must_not_introduce) and proof_for_view (what this view must make legible). camera tells you which end is nearer the camera and which side it sees.
- When the packet carries design, subject, locations and scenes instead, the vessel was not designed before the film: subject.profile is its detailed design, and the locations and scene actions are context, never stronger than the profile.

HOW TO WRITE

1. It is the same vessel. Never redesign it, never add a part, opening, level, count, direction or symmetry the source does not give, and never move a part to make it visible.
2. Write what this view needs: the geometry the requested camera reveals that the supplied image does not show, placed relative to the parts the image already shows, and a short restatement of the P0 invariants and signature regions this view shows, so that they do not drift. When proof_for_view is empty, those P0 invariants and signature regions are what this view must make legible.
3. Where the supplied image hides a part, take it from the design. A part the image hides but the design states is not a conflict. supplied_image_camera names the camera of the supplied image. Parts on its hidden faces are not shown in the image: never report how the image shows them, and take them from the design.
4. view_geometry selects the faces and sides this camera looks at; it is a focus, not proof of what is visible. Describe only what this camera can see, and do not drop a part merely because it is listed under landmarks or common_geometry.
5. Port and starboard are not mirror images unless the design says so. Describe the side the camera sees from that side's own rows; never copy or mirror the other side, and keep every rotation, offset and asymmetry in its stated direction.
6. Write one geometric relationship per sentence, in plain affirmative English, preferring relationships between parts over adjectives. Write each must_not_introduce as its instead, the shape that must be true, never as "no X".
7. A moving part appears only in its canonical state.
8. Name every part by the name, deck and side or face the design gives it. Ids such as d_main, o_salon_port or sr_skew_levels let you follow references; never write one.
9. Do not write camera, framing, lens, background, environment, lighting, colour, paint, finish or construction state, and do not say where a part sits in the frame; the application writes those blocks.
10. Keep view_geometry under 3500 characters and end it on a full sentence. Shorten by removing repetition, never by removing a fact this view needs.
11. Navigation and communication equipment is fitted at finishing and is not part of this reference: never describe it. Describe a mounting base the design lists as the plain structure it is; where the equipment will stand, the structure stays whole, never a hole or a cut-out. Never write guardrails, railings, guards or balustrades, even where the source mentions them inside another part's description; describe the edge they would stand on as a plain edge.
12. Where this camera sees the stern, keep its outline, open and closed boundaries, platforms and terrace levels, each stair's landings, deck levels, side, running direction and support, and each pool or recess on its deck and at its position, exactly as the source states them; never widen, narrow, close or reshape the stern, and never move a stair inside or outside the hull side. Keep the source's own shape words when they are clear, such as V-shaped, stepped or open; state where a V's point and arms lie only when the source says so, and never make a V-shaped terrace rectangular or an asymmetric part symmetric.
13. Read stem rake in its naval-architecture sense. A stem with forward rake leans forward as it rises: its top lies ahead of its foot, so going down from the waterline the stem moves aft toward the forefoot. "A near-plumb upper stem with a slight forward rake below the design waterline", "a lower stem that rakes slightly aft toward the forefoot" and "the below-water portion eases aft into the keel" therefore describe one shape, not a contradiction: a nearly vertical stem above the waterline whose lower part eases aft below it and turns into the keel. Write that shape by the direction the stem moves going down, for example "below the design waterline the lower stem eases slightly aft toward the forefoot", never as a bare "forward rake" or "aft rake". Only a source that says the lower stem projects, bulges or extends forward of the upper stem describes a stem that moves forward going down.

CONFLICTS

Add one plain-language line to conflicts, naming the parts by readable names, when:
- the supplied image clearly shows a part differently from the design: the number of masses, which masses connect, the overall proportions, a primary opening, a level, or the direction of a rotation or offset;
- the supplied image shows a state different from the bare-metal reference state, such as water in a pool, an applied finish, or a mast, an arch, a radar scanner, a satellite dome or other installed equipment; equipment that is missing from the supplied image is never a conflict. Dark or shadowed openings are never a conflict: the application's subject state already makes every opening that would hold glass an open cut-out with its frame;
- a fact this view needs is missing from the source, or two source statements disagree.
Never resolve a conflict in view_geometry and never write both versions. When there is nothing to report, conflicts is an empty array. Conflicts are shown to the user beside the prompt.

OUTPUT

Return only a JSON object with view_geometry, a string, and conflicts, an array of strings. No markdown fences, no commentary.
