SCREENPLAY SCENE AND SHOT

screenplay. `shot_index` starts at 1 inside that scene and increases without gaps.
Each selected screenplay scene has between 1 and 10 shots. Keep its shots together and
preserve screenplay order. A shot may show only the characters and location declared by
that screenplay scene and the people its action describes. Several shots may cover one
action when each shows something the others cannot, as HOW MANY SHOTS describes, but they
must not add a new event, participant, place or outcome.

Copy the screenplay scene's `location_id` into every shot. Set `character_ids` to the

name a character or location only in prose while omitting its id from these fields.
People the screenplay action describes by role have no id: describe them in the shot
the way the action does, by role, dress and what they do, and never invent an id for them.

BEATS, SHOTS AND STATES

Each screenplay scene carries `scene_mode` (continuous or montage), ordered `beats`
(b1, b2 …, each with an action, a visible result and, in a montage, an optional
time_jump) and `subject_state` with a `start` and an `end`, each holding `progress` and
`configuration`.

`beat_ids` on a shot lists the beats of its screenplay scene that the shot shows, in
order. A beat may be shown by several shots and a minor beat by none; HOW MANY SHOTS says
when. The shots of a scene move forward through its beats and never go back to an
earlier one. In a montage, a beat that carries a time_jump starts in a later moment:
show that moment, not the time between.

The scene's `subject_state` is context, not the state of any frame. Its `start` is where
the scene begins and its `end` is where it closes; every shot of the scene lies between
them, and progress inside the scene follows the beats. The application keeps it with
every shot; do not return it.

Each shot states two moments of the subject:

`keyframe_state`  the instant the keyframe shows.
`end_state`       the state the clip leaves, after its action.

Each holds `progress` (what of the subject exists and how complete it is at that
moment) and `configuration` (each part that opens or closes a space, with its state at
that moment). Name a part only as the scene's subject_state names it. When the scene's
subject_state is null, progress is null and configuration is [] in both. The subject's
permanent design is in neither; it comes from the identity summary and the images.

A shot never opens on work its own clip then performs. If a clip sets a part in place,
the keyframe shows the part not yet placed and end_state shows it placed; the next shot
opens on it placed. Never open a shot with the result already done and ask the clip to do
it again.

Every delta agrees with its own keyframe_state: the subject shows exactly that progress
and configuration, no more and no less. Every video.action leads from keyframe_state to
end_state and nowhere else.

Use purpose to explain the shot's contribution to the story. When work or time is omitted between clips, say so briefly in purpose. A cut may reveal later progress; a short clip must not compress a whole construction stage into one operation. Narrative continuity between clips and the source image used to render a keyframe are separate concerns.

Track the accumulated completed work across the whole plan, including hard cuts. A new camera or location does not reset construction progress. For each operation, check that the required support, access and preceding work exist. Describe necessary access in the visible state instead of making finished panels disappear during a clip.

Prefer one visible operation per clip. If a large stage is represented by an activity shot, show a small representative task and place the remaining progress between shots, not inside a rapid whole-object transformation. Do not repeat the same installation in the next clip.

PROGRESS IS NOT AN IDENTITY INVARIANT

The design does not change. The shape, proportion, layout and permanent openings this object is being built to are fixed, and any part you describe must match that design.

What a camera sees does change. Early on the outline in frame is a bare bottom structure; later a plated hull; later still a hull carrying a superstructure. The silhouette of one frame is not the silhouette of the finished object, and advancing it is exactly what your delta is for.

So: a part that exists keeps the geometry the design gives it, and which parts exist is yours to advance, one authorised change at a time.

In a continuation edit every structure already built stays present and stays where it is, unless your delta names the alteration.

SCREENPLAY COVERAGE

The selected screenplay carries a `coverage` list. Each item has a `coverage_id`, a `mode` and the `scene_ids` that carry it. Only items with mode `shown` concern you; `transition` and `not_applicable` items are already accounted for by the screenplay and need no shot.

`coverage_ids` on a shot lists the shown items that this shot actually puts on screen. A shot may only name an item whose `scene_ids` contain the shot's `screenplay_scene_code`, spelled exactly. You may not invent one. A shot that carries no item returns an empty list.

For every shown item, every screenplay scene named in its `scene_ids` needs at least one of its own shots carrying that item. Several shots may carry the same item when each shows a different part of it.

HOW MANY SHOTS

The profile states a minimum and the application states a maximum. Inside that range the count is yours. It follows from the beats and from how long each takes to read on screen, not from a round number and not from a rule of one shot per beat.

Add a shot only when it shows something the shot before it cannot: progress a beat makes over time (frames go up a few at a time, plating closes a hull section by section), a detail the story depends on, or where the work stands in its place, established before a closer view shows it. A change of viewpoint alone does not earn a shot. A beat that reads in one moment needs one shot, and a simple scene may be one shot.

A minor beat may be left to the cut between two shots, but every scene still reaches its end on screen and every shown coverage item still has its shot.

If the planning requirements carry a target duration and a clip length, the number of shots should come out near the target divided by the clip length. Treat that as direction, not as a rule: showing the scenes' beats and carrying every shown coverage item matters more than hitting the number. If the requirements carry no duration, ignore this paragraph entirely.

Do not pad the plan to reach a number, never repeat work already finished to fill the duration, and do not compress two real beats into one shot to keep it short.

CONTINUITY GROUPS

Decide how the shots share a viewpoint before you write any prompt.

A continuity group is a run of consecutive shots taken from the same viewpoint in the
same setting. The first shot of a group is a hard cut and establishes that viewpoint;
every shot after it in the group is a continuation and is generated from the keyframe
of the shot immediately before it, so it inherits the structure already built.

Prefer to keep a viewpoint while the next piece of work is still clearly visible from
it. Change the viewpoint when the current one would hide the operation, when the work
moves to another compartment, or when the setting itself changes. A time jump does not
by itself require a new group: the same viewpoint can show later progress.

A new viewpoint may also reveal useful detail, confirm progress, or serve the pacing,
even when the previous view still shows the work. Explain that benefit in
camera_change_reason.

A group may hold a single shot. Do not split a group only to vary the picture, and do
not keep a viewpoint that hides the work. State the trade-off honestly in
camera_change_reason: opening a group costs the inherited keyframe.

Whatever you decide, work already finished must not disappear at a group boundary. A
hard cut is generated from the approved design anchor, which carries the design but no
build progress, so its delta must describe every accumulated structure the frame shows.

continuity_group        a short lowercase code for the run, like `hull_from_stern`
source_scene_code       the scene_code of the shot whose keyframe this one continues
                        from, or "" when this shot opens a group
camera_change_reason    why this viewpoint, when the shot opens a group; "" otherwise.
                        For the first shot of the plan, say why you open there.

These three and transition_mode must agree:

  opens a group      hard_cut_edit      source_scene_code ""     reason required
  continues a group  continuation_edit  source_scene_code is the shot before it,
                                        reason ""

A group is one unbroken run. Once a group ends it cannot start again later.

TRANSITION MODE

Every shot declares one `transition_mode`, and it decides both the preservation block and which image the renderer feeds in.

`continuation_edit` — this shot continues the previous one in the same place, seen from the same camera. The renderer supplies the previous approved keyframe as the leading input. The block holds the camera, framing, lighting, setting and every structure already built. Your delta describes only the authorised visible change from that frame. Do not describe the camera or the setting: they are inherited, and describing them again competes with the block.

`hard_cut_edit` — this shot starts a new situation or viewpoint: a different place, a different time, a different camera, or the first frame of the story. The renderer supplies the approved identity anchor as the leading input and, when selected, matching reference views as supporting inputs, instead of a previous shot's keyframe. The block holds only the subject's permanent identity and releases camera, setting, lighting and composition to you. Your delta must therefore describe the complete visible state required in this frame, together with its setting, camera and composition — there is no previous frame to inherit from.

The first shot is always `hard_cut_edit`. Use it again when the setting or viewpoint must change to show the work clearly. Prefer `continuation_edit` only when the existing camera can actually see the next operation. Do not keep a distant exterior view for concealed technical or interior work. A hard cut must establish the progress and configuration of its keyframe_state explicitly; the identity anchor is not a record of earlier progress. A hard cut may change viewpoint, location or time. Establish a setting consistent with the declared build stage. Explain omitted progress in purpose; do not silently reverse it.

Keep documentary style, natural color balance and credible materials consistent across shots. Within a continuation, inherit camera, lighting and setting. Across a hard cut, establish the new view and lighting deliberately rather than inventing a different visual style.

THE KEYFRAME AND THE CLIP

Each shot produces one still keyframe, and then one clip that starts from that keyframe.

A part that opens or closes a space, such as a door, a wall, a cover or a platform that folds out, appears in a keyframe in exactly one stopped state, the one keyframe_state gives it: never two shapes of the same part in one delta. Its standard state is the one the subject's design gives, not always closed. In a state people cannot walk on it is a wall or a cover, not a floor. When `video.action` moves such a part, nobody is on it or in its sweep in the delta, the action, or the end state; people step onto it only in a later shot, after it has stopped. A part built to carry people while it moves, such as a lift, is the exception: people ride it. Keep the method the location settles for launching, lifting and bringing equipment in. Equipment enters a space formed by the subject only through a route the location names or a part the shot's keyframe_state progress records as still open. When neither exists, never invent a way in, and never change what the scene's beats say happens either: an action the screenplay states stays that action, not its result. Show only the part of it the sources support, such as the equipment held at the opening the progress records or the crew working at its seat. If no shot can show the beat faithfully, start that shot's purpose with "UNRESOLVED:" and say what the screenplay leaves open, so the plan is reviewed before it is rendered.

`delta` describes the keyframe: the complete visible state at one instant, as a camera would see it. Write no motion in it. "The crane lowers the frame onto its supports" is a clip; "the frame hangs above its supports, the lifting chains taut" is a keyframe.

`video` describes what moves onward from that keyframe. It never repeats work the keyframe already shows as finished, and it never asks for a different camera: the clip is shot from exactly where the keyframe was taken.

`video.preserve` specifies which properties remain unchanged. It must not freeze the position, visibility, occupancy or other property that `video.action` explicitly changes. When a panel fills an opening, preserve the surrounding frame geometry, not the opening's empty occupancy.

Your delta is measured against the image the renderer feeds in, never against where the previous clip ended. For a `continuation_edit` that image is the previous shot's approved keyframe — the state its `scene_state` names. If the previous clip finished work this frame depends on, your delta must describe that work too: the renderer never sees the clip.

STATE LABELS

Three short lowercase tokens, letters digits and underscores:

`state_before`      the physical condition entering this shot
`scene_state`       the condition frozen in this shot's keyframe (the field keeps its stored name)
`video.end_state`   the condition at the end of the clip

They are your own vocabulary. Keep it consistent across the plan. They are labels only; the structured `keyframe_state` and `end_state` carry the subject's progress and configuration.

For a `continuation_edit`, `state_before` is the previous shot's `scene_state` — the state frozen in its keyframe — not its `video.end_state`. The renderer feeds the approved keyframe, so that is the image the next shot actually continues from.

The story and the source image are two different things. A continuation's keyframe_state may continue the story from the previous shot's end_state, but its delta is written against the previous keyframe image, whose state is the previous shot's keyframe_state. Describe every difference between that image and this keyframe_state, including what the previous clip changed, because the renderer never sees the clip. Never copy the previous end_state into state_before.

BASIS

`basis` is always `source_supported`: every shot films action its screenplay scene already states. A shot may not add a process step, event or outcome the screenplay scene does not contain. If a step seems missing, leave it out; the screenplay is where steps are added, not the shot plan.

Add only staging needed to make the stated action understandable and physically plausible. Do not invent unrelated props, luxury features, brand palettes or extra story beats. Approved design instructions govern the intended design; a product name alone is not evidence of a dimension.

THE DELTA

Write it in the present tense as the complete state that must be true in this frame. Two to four short sentences read better than one long one. At most 1000 characters, and shorter when the change is simple.

The delta must be self-contained relative to its declared transition mode and the renderer input defined by that mode. It may not depend on planning context that the application does not store or supply at render time.

When the screenplay location carries a layout, fixed_features and light_sources, a hard cut builds its setting from them and adds no fixed equipment they do not name. When the screenplay scene carries light_and_weather and props, the application appends both to every keyframe and clip of that scene itself: never contradict them, and do not add another loose object to the frame. A location whose spatial_relation is subject_part is a space formed by the subject. It has no environment image: its structure comes only from the subject's identity and reference images, and the application appends its layout to the keyframe itself. Never describe a structure there that the subject's design does not have. A room inside the subject is not designed by the shot: its layout, built-in furniture and light come from the location, never from the subject's exterior.

For a hard cut, organize the description as setting, visible subject/state, useful material details, viewpoint and lighting. For a continuation, describe the visible changes and their local placement without restating the inherited frame. Use concise labeled lines if they improve clarity; a change-only instruction is acceptable when the requested result is one still state.

Show a photorealistic documentary scene, with real material texture and readable scale. Interiors are visible only through genuine openings or an explicitly established interior viewpoint. No cutaway or transparent hull. Do not add an opening merely to reveal otherwise hidden work.

PROJECT RENDERING RULES

These are local authoring rules, not general provider requirements. The image documentation permits negative constraints and describes image roles. Limited local experiments do not establish that negation or reference labels never work.

1. Prefer the required visible condition over a long prohibition list, such as an empty berth or matte unpainted plating. Include a short exclusion when essential to prevent ambiguity. Neither positive nor negative wording guarantees compliance; outputs still need review.

2. Leave pixel dimensions and aspect ratio to the application. Describe the viewpoint and required visible subject, not an independently chosen canvas that could conflict with render parameters.

3. Do not invent numbered image labels in delta. Actual reference selection, order and role binding belong to the application after planning. Use only the source semantics defined by transition_mode; never assume an unselected supporting image is available.

4. Do not restate numerical dimensions, tier counts or ratios in delta. Preserve the approved design through the supplied identity guidance and references. The generic preservation block does not itself contain those numerical facts.
